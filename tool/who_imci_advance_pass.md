# WHO Pocket Book — full advance pass, all 258 topics

**Scope: every topic in the book.** Chapter 8 (HIV, 34 topics) was reviewed and applied on
2026-08-25/27. This file covers **the remaining 224**, chapter by chapter, and is committed
per chapter so it can go to OE in batches rather than waiting for the whole book.

**Setting to state to OE:** *district hospital, Gulu, northern Uganda — low-resource,
paediatric-heavy, malaria-endemic. Limited drug availability; no reliable dialysis; ICU
monitoring is not routinely available.*

**Format:** every item is self-contained — verbatim printed text, the concern, a specific
question. Nothing refers to a file or a previous conversation, because OE cannot see them.
Ask for figures as **standalone lines**.

**Marks:** 🔴 patient-safety · 🟡 correctness/clarity · 🟢 minor · ✅ checked, no change.

**Context that matters for this book specifically:** the print is the **2nd edition, 2013**,
and **240 of 258 topics already carry an OpenEvidence review from 2026-07**. So unlike the
ProtocolBook, most content here has been through one pass. That does **not** make it
current — chapter 8 was reviewed in 2026-07 and still lacked the AMBITION regimen, the
HIV-TB pathway and the abacavir screen. The job here is to find what the first pass missed.

# CHAPTER 7 — SEVERE ACUTE MALNUTRITION (27 topics, pp.197–223)

**Overall: this chapter is in good shape.** The 2026-07 review was thorough and is visibly
current — it carries the 2023 WHO revised energy target, the Trehan/Isanaka context for
routine antibiotics, Bandsma 2019 on modified F-75, phosphorus-fortified F-75, the vitamin
A day-15 correction, the no-iron-with-RUTF rule, and the TRACT-informed transfusion
volumes. Doses were extracted and checked one by one across all 27 topics; **the numbers
are internally consistent and match WHO** (F-75 volumes reconcile to 130 ml/kg/day at every
frequency; vitamin A doses agree between §7.4.6 and §7.5.1; potassium 3–4 and magnesium
0.4–0.6 mmol/kg/day as printed).

**One item needs OE.**

## 7-1 🟡 The RUTF table still labels its columns with the superseded 2013 targets

**Verbatim, as printed.** A callout explains that the 2023 WHO guideline lowered the
catch-up energy target from 150–220 kcal/kg/day to **150–185** while still severely
malnourished, then **100–130** until discharge, and adds: *"The packet table below reflects
the 2013 dosing — read its lower (transition) column as the practical rehabilitation amount
under the revised target."*

The table immediately below it is headed:

> **RUTF per day (92 g packets, 500 kcal each)** — columns *"Weight (kg)"*,
> *"Transition (150 kcal/kg/d)"*, *"Rehab (200 kcal/kg/d)"*

**The concern.** The reconciliation lives in the prose; the **table still says "Rehab
(200 kcal/kg/d)"**. A table is what gets read at speed at the bedside, and the column
labelled "Rehab" carries a target the guideline has withdrawn. A reader who goes straight
to the table — the normal way a packet count is looked up — is given the superseded number
under the more authoritative-sounding heading.

**Questions.**
1. Under the 2023 targets, what are the **correct RUTF packet counts per day** for each
   weight band, at **150–185 kcal/kg/day** and at **100–130 kcal/kg/day**? Please give the
   two columns as a table, using 92 g / 500 kcal packets, for the bands 4.0–4.9, 5.0–6.9,
   7.0–8.4, 8.5–9.4, 9.5–10.4, 10.5–11.9 and ≥ 12.0 kg.
2. Should the columns be labelled by **phase** (transition / rehabilitation) or by
   **energy target**? Which is less likely to be misread?
3. Is there any circumstance in which the old 200 kcal/kg/day amount is still correct — and
   if not, should that column simply be removed rather than relabelled?

# CHAPTER 1 — TRIAGE & EMERGENCY CONDITIONS (33 topics, pp.1–40)

**Overall: the strongest chapter in the book.** The 2026-07 review is visibly current and
in places better than the guideline text — it carries FEAST-informed cautious fluids,
GASTROSAM, the Phoenix sepsis score, SugarFACT for the 2.5 mmol/L threshold, EAST-2025 TXA
with its maintenance infusion, buccal/intranasal midazolam as first-line for seizures, the
phenytoin ≤ 1 mg/kg/min rate, and a **rejected** reviewer suggestion (70 → 100 ml/kg in
Chart 11) with the arithmetic worked out to show why the original was right. Every dose in
all 33 topics was extracted and checked; the weight-band tables reconcile to their stated
per-kg rates throughout.

**Three items need OE. The first is the most serious finding of this pass so far.**

## 1-1 🔴 The two books in this app give a child under 40 kg DIFFERENT paracetamol antidote regimens

**This is an internal contradiction between the two books the app ships, on an antidote.**

**What the WHO book prints** (§1.6.4, specific poisons):
> **"≤ 40 kg — three-bag: 150 mg/kg over 15 min, then 50 mg/kg over 4 h, then 100 mg/kg
> over 16 h."**
> **"> 40 kg — two-bag (fewer anaphylactoid reactions): 200 mg/kg over 4 h, then 100 mg/kg
> over 16 h."**
> with a note that *"the FDA states it is not established whether the two-bag regimen is as
> effective as three-bag."*

**What the other book in the same app prints**, in a table headed **"N-acetylcysteine (NAC)
— 2-bag regimen, ALL weights"**:
> **Child ≤ 40 kg:** 200 mg/kg in 7 mL/kg diluent over 4 h, then 100 mg/kg in 14 mL/kg over 16 h
> **> 40 kg:** 200 mg/kg (max 22 g) over 4 h, then 100 mg/kg (max 11 g) over 16 h

So a 30 kg child with paracetamol poisoning gets **three-bag** if the clinician opens one
book and **two-bag** if they open the other, in the same app, on the same ward.

**Relevant history.** This exact question was put to OpenEvidence for the second book and
answered: **"2-bag for all weights — the FDA wording is absent data, not harm."** That
ruling was applied there. The WHO volume still carries the three-bag regimen for children
**and** cites the same FDA wording as its reason.

**Questions.**
1. Does the **two-bag regimen apply to children under 40 kg** as well as adults? Please
   answer yes or no on its own line.
2. If yes, please give the **complete paediatric two-bag regimen** — mg/kg and diluent
   volume for each bag, and the infusion time for each — as standalone lines.
3. If there is a **weight below which** two-bag should not be used, state it as a
   standalone line.
4. Is the three-bag regimen ever preferable in a child — for example late presentation,
   massive ingestion, or established hepatotoxicity?
5. The FDA "not established" wording is the stated reason for keeping three-bag in
   children. Is that an absence of trial data rather than evidence of inferiority, and
   should it change practice?

## 1-2 🟡 The book prints an IV glucose dose 2.5× the paediatric standard and flags the conflict without resolving it

**Verbatim, as printed** (Chart 10, how to give glucose intravenously):
> *"Give 5 ml/kg of 10% glucose rapidly by IV injection"* — with a weight table
> (< 4 kg → 15 ml, 4–6 kg → 25 ml, 6–10 kg → 40 ml, 10–14 kg → 60 ml, 14–19 kg → 80 ml)

and, alongside it, the book's own note:
> *"WHO uses 5 ml/kg (0.5 g/kg) — about 2.5× the PES / AAP / APLS standard of 2 ml/kg
> (0.2 g/kg)."*

**The concern.** The page states the conflict honestly but still **instructs the higher
dose**, leaving the clinician to choose at the moment they are treating a hypoglycaemic
child. Both figures are on the same page and they differ 2.5-fold.

**Questions.**
1. For a hypoglycaemic child, which should a pocket reference **instruct** — 5 ml/kg or
   2 ml/kg of 10% glucose? Please give the recommended figure on its own line.
2. Does the answer differ for **neonates**, for **severe acute malnutrition**, or by
   severity of hypoglycaemia? If so, give each as a standalone line.
3. Is there evidence that the higher dose causes harm (rebound hypoglycaemia, osmotic
   load), or that the lower dose under-treats in a resource-limited setting where a repeat
   glucose measurement may be delayed?
4. What **maintenance infusion** should follow the bolus, in mg/kg/min?

## 1-3 🟡 Prophylactic adrenaline before antivenom — is it still recommended?

**Verbatim, as printed** (§1.9.1, snake bite): *"Premedication: prophylactic SC adrenaline
0.005 mg/kg (≈ 0.005 ml/kg of 1:1000) in a child — the 0.25 ml / 0.25 mg figure is an adult
/ older-child dose."*

**The concern.** The book has corrected the **dose** carefully, but I am not sure the
**practice** is still recommended. Giving adrenaline prophylactically to every child before
antivenom is a significant intervention, and my impression is that guidelines differ on it.

**Questions.**
1. Is **prophylactic subcutaneous adrenaline before antivenom** still recommended? Yes or
   no on its own line, and please say which body recommends or advises against it.
2. If it is recommended, is it for **all** patients receiving antivenom or only
   higher-risk ones — and what defines higher risk?
3. If it is **not** recommended, what should be prepared instead, and how quickly must
   adrenaline be available once the infusion starts?
4. Please confirm the correct paediatric prophylactic dose in **mg/kg**, so the book's
   0.005 mg/kg can be checked.

# CHAPTER 3 — YOUNG INFANTS & NEONATES (24 topics, pp.47–76)

**Overall: thoroughly reviewed and current.** The 2026-07 pass carries 2025 AHA/AAP and ERC
guidance on cord milking, OMWaNA on kangaroo care, the CAP/FDA caffeine maintenance
ceiling, 2022 WHO chlorhexidine cord care, and — importantly — **it already avoids
ceftriaxone in neonates on the meningitis page**, preferring cefotaxime because of bilirubin
displacement and calcium precipitation. Every dose across all 24 topics was extracted and
checked; the neonatal drug table's per-kg figures reconcile with its volume columns, and
the day-1 fluid figures that look contradictory are in fact correctly separated (the table
is for term / low-birth-weight infants, the 80–90 ml/kg figure is flagged as **very**
preterm / VLBW).

**Two items need OE, and they are the same drug.**

## 3-1 🔴 The same eye infection is given two different ceftriaxone maximums

**Verbatim, as printed.** The neonatal conjunctivitis page:
> *"Give **ceftriaxone 25–50 mg/kg (maximum 250 mg)** IM/IV as a single dose."*

The neonatal drug-dose table, a few pages later, under ceftriaxone:
> *"**Pus from eye: 50 mg/kg IM once (max 125 mg)**"*

**The concern.** One book, one indication — gonococcal ophthalmia in a newborn — and two
maximum doses that differ twofold. A 4 kg baby at 50 mg/kg is 200 mg: allowed by one page,
above the cap on the other. Whichever page the clinician happens to open decides the dose.

**Questions.**
1. What is the correct single-dose ceftriaxone **maximum** for gonococcal ophthalmia
   neonatorum? Please give it in mg on its own line.
2. What is the correct **mg/kg** figure — 25–50 mg/kg, or a flat 50 mg/kg? Standalone line.
3. Does the maximum differ between **prophylaxis** and **treatment** of eye infection, and
   is that the origin of the two figures?

## 3-2 🟡 Ceftriaxone is avoided in neonates on one page and given to them on another

**The concern.** The neonatal **meningitis** page states plainly that ceftriaxone should be
avoided in neonates — bilirubin displacement and calcium precipitation — and directs the
reader to cefotaxime instead. The neonatal **conjunctivitis** page then gives *ceftriaxone*
as the first choice, with cefotaxime 100 mg/kg only as the alternative. Both pages are
about newborns, in the same chapter.

I can see a defensible reason — a single small IM dose is not the same exposure as a
meningitis course — but the book does not say that, so the two pages simply read as
contradicting each other.

**Questions.**
1. Is a **single** IM dose of ceftriaxone acceptable in a neonate for gonococcal
   ophthalmia, despite the general caution? Yes or no on its own line.
2. Does the answer change in a **jaundiced** neonate, or one receiving **calcium-containing
   fluids**? Please answer each separately.
3. If ceftriaxone must be avoided in those infants, is **cefotaxime 100 mg/kg** the right
   substitute and is one dose sufficient?
4. What one sentence should the conjunctivitis page carry so it does not read as
   contradicting the meningitis page?

## 3-3 🟡 The book uses three different hypoglycaemia thresholds — is the neonatal one current?

**Verbatim, as printed**, across the book:
- Young-infant danger signs: *"**< 2.2 mmol/L (< 40 mg/100 ml)**, give 10% glucose 2 ml/kg IV"*
- Chart 10 (children): *"**< 2.5 mmol/L (45 mg/dL)**, or **< 3 mmol/L** in a severely malnourished child"*
- Severe malaria: *"blood glucose **< 2.5 mmol/L**"*

**The concern.** Three thresholds is defensible if they are genuinely age- and
context-specific, and the malnutrition one clearly is. But **2.2 mmol/L for a neonate is
lower than the operational threshold I have seen used** in neonatal practice, and it is the
lowest number in the book applied to the most vulnerable patients.

**Questions.**
1. What is the current **operational threshold for treating hypoglycaemia in a neonate**?
   Give the figure in mmol/L on its own line.
2. Does it differ in the **first 48 hours** versus later, or for **at-risk** infants
   (small-for-gestational-age, infant of a diabetic mother, preterm)? Please give each as a
   standalone line.
3. The book gives neonates **2 ml/kg** of 10% glucose and older children **5 ml/kg**. Is
   that difference correct, and is 2 ml/kg the right neonatal bolus?

## 4-1 🟡 The two books give different daily co-trimoxazole doses for PCP

**Verbatim, as printed.** This book, in two places that agree with each other:
> §4.2.1 severe pneumonia: *"add **high-dose co-trimoxazole (8 mg/kg trimethoprim + 40 mg/kg
> sulfamethoxazole) every 8 h for 3 weeks**"*
> §8.4.2 PCP: *"**High-dose co-trimoxazole (8 mg/kg TMP / 40 mg/kg SMX)** oral or preferably
> **IV, three times daily for 3 weeks**"*

That is **24 mg/kg/day of trimethoprim.**

The other book in this app, for the same disease:
> *"TMP-SMX: TMP **5 mg/kg/dose** & SMX 25 mg/kg/dose IV or PO **q6hrs**"* for 21 days

That is **20 mg/kg/day of trimethoprim.**

**The concern.** Same app, same disease, two daily totals. 24 mg/kg/day is also **above the
15–20 mg/kg/day range** I associate with PCP treatment, and co-trimoxazole at high dose
carries real marrow, renal and hypersensitivity toxicity — particularly in the
HIV-positive children this regimen is aimed at.

**Questions.**
1. What is the correct **trimethoprim total daily dose** for PCP treatment in a child, in
   mg/kg/day? Standalone line.
2. Should it be divided **three times daily or four times daily**? Standalone line.
3. Is **24 mg/kg/day** ever correct, or is 15–20 mg/kg/day the ceiling?
4. Does the answer differ between **IV and oral**, or by severity?
5. What monitoring does the high-dose course require, and for how long?

# CHAPTER 5 — DIARRHOEA (9 topics, pp.125–142)

✅ **No change needed.** All 9 topics read and every dose checked. The 2026-07 review is
current: low-osmolarity ORS as the standard, the NEJM 2020 evidence that **5 mg and 10 mg
zinc are non-inferior to 20 mg with less vomiting** (with 20 mg correctly retained as the
WHO dose), azithromycin as preferred cholera treatment, the GASTRO slow-rehydration
evidence noted alongside Plan C, and metronidazole reserved for confirmed indications
rather than given empirically.

Two things that look like contradictions are **correctly distinguished** and were checked
rather than assumed: the two ceftriaxone regimens in the dysentery topic are **50–80
mg/kg/day for dysentery** versus **100 mg/kg for suspected sepsis in a young infant**, which
is right; and the Plan C volumes reconcile with Chart 11 (100 ml/kg = 30 fast + 70).

⚠️ **One observation that strengthens flag 3-2 rather than opening a new one:** this chapter
also gives **ceftriaxone 100 mg/kg to neonates and young infants** for suspected sepsis,
while the neonatal meningitis page avoids ceftriaxone in neonates entirely. That is now the
**third** page in the book giving a neonate ceftriaxone against that caution. Whatever OE
answers for 3-2 should be applied to all three.
