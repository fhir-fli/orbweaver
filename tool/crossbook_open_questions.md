## 1 🔴 Neonatal meningitis — which ampicillin dose?

Two paediatric references used side by side give different ampicillin regimens for a
neonate with meningitis, and three different daily totals are in circulation.

**Reference A** treats meningitis in the young infant with "ampicillin + gentamicin",
and its only ampicillin entry is a neonatal drug-dose table reading:
> **Ampicillin — 50 mg/kg IM/IV: week 1 every 12 h; weeks 2–4 every 8 h**

That is **100 mg/kg/day in week 1 and 150 mg/kg/day thereafter**. The table has no
separate meningitis row, and the meningitis page does not say to increase it.

**Reference B** prints, for a neonate:
> First line: ampicillin 50 mg/kg q8h (q12h in the 1st week) + gentamicin
> **If concern for meningitis: ampicillin 100 mg/kg q8h (q12h in the 1st week)** plus
> cefotaxime 50 mg/kg q8h (q12h in the 1st week)

That is **200 mg/kg/day in week 1 and 300 mg/kg/day thereafter** — it explicitly doubles
the dose when meningitis is suspected. The same reference gives **ampicillin 75 mg/kg IV
6-hourly (300 mg/kg/day)** for Listeria in the older child.

**A third figure has also been quoted** for neonatal meningitis: 100 mg/kg/day in week 1
and 150 mg/kg/day in weeks 2–4, cited to the FDA label — the same numbers as the
septicaemia dose above.

**The concern.** Ampicillin is the Listeria and group B streptococcus drug, the CSF is
the compartment that matters, and 150 mg/kg/day is the dose intended for bacteraemia
without meningitis. A clinician gets 150 or 300 mg/kg/day depending on which book is
open.

**Questions.** Please give each figure as a **standalone line with no citation after the
number**.
1. **Confirmed or suspected neonatal meningitis, ≤ 7 days old:** ___ mg/kg/day, and the
   dosing interval.
2. **8–28 days old:** ___ mg/kg/day, and the dosing interval.
3. Is **150 mg/kg/day** adequate for neonatal meningitis, or is it a septicaemia dose
   that under-treats the CSF?
4. Is **300 mg/kg/day** in a neonate too much, or is it the right Listeria and group B
   streptococcus cover?
5. What is the correct **cefotaxime** dose for neonatal meningitis over the same two age
   windows?
6. Should a neonatal drug-dose table carry a **separate meningitis row** for ampicillin
   and cefotaxime, rather than one sepsis dose?

## 2 🟡 Child TB contact — 6 months of isoniazid, or 3 months of isoniazid + rifampicin?

**What one reference prints**, on its TB diagnosis page:
> Well child contacts who do NOT have TB: give **isoniazid preventive therapy 10 mg/kg
> daily for 6 months**.

**What the other reference prints**, on its tuberculosis page:
> **3HR — daily isoniazid + rifampicin × 3 months (all ages).**

**The concern.** Both are WHO-endorsed TB preventive regimens, but they are different
drugs for different lengths, and neither page mentions the other. A child contact gets
six months of one drug or three months of two depending on which book is open. Adherence
over six months in a district-hospital catchment is the whole problem TPT has, and the
shorter rifamycin-based regimens exist because of it.

**Questions.**
1. For a **well child household contact of a smear-positive case**, which regimen should
   a pocket reference print — **6H**, **3HR**, **3HP**, or **1HP**? Answer on its own
   line, with no citation after it.
2. Does the answer change for a **child living with HIV**, or one **under 5 years**?
3. Is **6H still acceptable** where rifapentine or the fixed-dose combinations are not
   stocked — which is the usual situation here?
4. Give the **dose and duration** of the recommended regimen as standalone lines.

## 3 🟡 Oxygen — start at SpO₂ < 94% or < 90%?

**One reference prints `Start oxygen if SpO₂ < 94%`** on at least fourteen adult
pages — sepsis, meningitis, heart failure, DKA, ACS, liver failure, convulsions,
anaphylaxis, hypertension, alcohol intoxication and withdrawal, epistaxis, anaemia and
paediatric DKA. Its own **paediatric pneumonia** page uses **< 90%**.

**The other reference uses < 90%** as the threshold to give oxygen, throughout.

**The concern.** Oxygen is the most rationed resource in this setting. A 94% trigger
starts oxygen on a substantially larger group than a 90% trigger, and the two books
disagree by four points on the same monitor. The first is also internally split:
94% for adults, 90% for children.

**Questions.**
1. What SpO₂ threshold should a district hospital in northern Uganda print for
   **starting oxygen in an adult**? One line, no citation after the number.
2. What threshold for a **child**? One line.
3. Is a **94%** trigger defensible where oxygen supply is limited, or does it consume
   supply without benefit?
4. Should the printed **target** differ from the printed **trigger** — and what target
   should be printed for a patient at risk of hypercapnia?
